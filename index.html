Chapter 1 — The Network

Australia's Spine of Steel

Over 33,000 kilometres of railway crisscross a continent the size of Europe. This network carries everything from iron ore across the Pilbara to commuters through Sydney's tunnels — yet it remains largely invisible to the average Australian.

The map below plots every major rail corridor colour-coded by track gauge. Standard-gauge lines (amber) form the interstate spine. Victoria's broad-gauge (blue) network is the country's most intensively used. Queensland's vast narrow-gauge (red) system stretches north to Cairns and west to Mt Isa. Hover any line for details.

Chapter 2 — Passengers

Cities in Motion

Urban heavy rail is how Australians get to work. In 2022–23, the five major capital city networks carried over 627 million passengers — equivalent to every Australian catching the train 24 times.

Sydney dominates, carrying more than the other four cities combined. But the fastest-growing story is Perth: its METRONET expansion keeps pushing patronage higher even as Melbourne and Sydney still recover from the pandemic.

Chapter 3 — The COVID Cliff

When the Trains
Ran Empty

In March 2020, Australia locked down. Rail patronage collapsed overnight. Sydney lost 39% of its riders in 2019–20, then another 37% in 2020–21. Melbourne fell even harder. The trains kept running — but almost no one was on board.

The monthly heatmap reveals the full story. The abrupt collapse in March–April 2020 is unmistakable. Notice also the regular seasonal dips every January and July — school holidays — and how that rhythm gradually returned as the city came back to life.

Chapter 4 — Reliability

On Time, or Off Track?

Every delayed train is a missed meeting, a late school pickup, a lost hour. Australians expect their trains to run on time — but performance varies dramatically across operators and years.

The heatmap maps each operator's on-time performance across five years. Deep red means chronic underperformance; teal means reliability. Regional services — V/Line and NSW TrainLink — consistently sit in the red. The lollipop chart lets you explore year by year: the white ring is the published target, and colour shows whether it was met.

Chapter 5 — Freight

The Invisible Railway

While passenger trains command the headlines, Australia's freight railways are among the busiest on Earth. The Pilbara heavy-haul lines run trains over 2.5 km long, 24 hours a day, seven days a week — mostly invisible to the Australian public.

The two Nightingale roses below break down freight exports by port for coal and iron ore — Australia's two largest rail freight commodities. Each petal's outward reach encodes the volume. Port Hedland, handling over half a billion tonnes of iron ore every year, dwarfs every other port in the country.

Chapter 6 — The Gauge Wars

A Nation Divided
By Millimetres

In 1854, Victoria opened Australia's first steam railway on broad gauge (1,600 mm) track. Five years later, NSW opened on standard gauge (1,435 mm). Queensland chose narrow gauge (1,067 mm) for cheaper construction. Three colonies. Three gauges. A century of broken journeys.

The waffle chart makes the proportions visceral: each square represents roughly 1% of Australia's total rail network. More than half the country's rail kilometres are narrow gauge. Standard gauge carries nearly all interstate traffic. Broad gauge is almost entirely Victoria.

Waffle — Network km by gauge (each cell ≈ 1%)

100 cells = 100% of Australia's ~27,840 km rail network. Narrow gauge (red, 55 cells), standard gauge (amber, 29 cells), broad gauge (blue, 16 cells).
Source: BITRE Trainline 12, 2025.

The long road to gauge unification

1854
Victoria opens first railway on broad gauge, 1,600 mm.
1855
NSW switches to standard gauge — incompatible with Victoria from the start.
1865
Queensland adopts narrow gauge (1,067 mm) to cut construction costs.
1917
Trans-Australian railway opens, linking Perth to the east on standard gauge.
1962
Sydney–Melbourne converted to standard gauge — first direct service in 107 years.
1995
Melbourne–Adelaide converted: the interstate standard-gauge spine is finally complete.
Today
Queensland's break-of-gauge at the NSW border remains. Direct Sydney–Brisbane rail is still not possible.
Chapter 7 — Building the Future

The Billion-Dollar
Building Boom

Australia is in the midst of its largest rail construction program in history. ABS data shows railway engineering construction reached AU$17 billion in 2023–24 — a historic high, and more than triple the 2015 figure of $5.2 billion.

The dumbbell chart strips away year-by-year noise to show total railway infrastructure spend from 1996 to 2024. The bump in the mid-2000s reflects the mining boom. The steep climb after 2015 reflects the urban megaprojects: Sydney Metro, METRONET, and Melbourne Airport Rail.

Dumbbell — Railway engineering construction: 1996 vs 2024 (AU$ billion)

Each dot = one year's total railway engineering construction work done (AU$ million, nominal). The dumbbell highlights the start (1996) and end (2024) anchors. Railway investment in 2024 is 4.7× the 1996 level in nominal terms.
Source: ABS Engineering Construction Activity, Australia (cat. no. 8762.0), accessed via BITRE Yearbook 2025.
