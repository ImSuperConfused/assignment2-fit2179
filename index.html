Chapter 1 — The Network

The Network
Nobody Sees

Every day, trains cross deserts nobody visits, tunnels nobody walks through, and bridges nobody photographs. Australia's 33,000-kilometre railway is one of the most extensive networks on Earth — yet for most Australians, it exists only as a morning commute. The ore trains, the interstate freight, the regional services running through empty scrubland: all invisible.

The map below makes it visible. Every major corridor is colour-coded by track gauge — the distance between the two rails. Amber lines trace the standard-gauge interstate spine connecting Sydney to Perth and Adelaide to Darwin. Blue lines reveal Victoria's dense broad-gauge suburban and regional web. Red lines show Queensland's sprawling narrow-gauge network, reaching north to Cairns and west to Mt Isa. The green lines threading through the Pilbara are something different entirely — private heavy-haul railways owned by BHP and Rio Tinto, running some of the longest and heaviest trains on Earth.

Chapter 2 — The Passengers

Five Cities,
Five Stories

Urban heavy rail is the circulatory system of Australia's capital cities. In 2023-24, the five major networks carried a combined 627 million passengers — but the distribution of those trips were anything but evenly. Sydney alone accounts for more than half of every journey taken.

The map below tells the story of scale: Sydney's circle dwarfs every other city. But scale is not the same as momentum. The connected path chart traces each city's patronage trajectory from 2010 to 2023 — and Perth's path stands apart. While Melbourne and Sydney trace arcs that climb, collapse, and struggle to return, Perth's line keeps moving forward. Its METRONET expansion has opened route after route, carrying new riders who never previously had a train option. By 2023-24, Perth was the only major capital city to have surpassed its pre-pandemic patronage peak.

Chapter 3 — The Peaks & Crashes

Riding High,
Then the Crash

From 2010 to 2019, Australia's rail networks were on a winning streak. Sydney grew from 294 million trips to 379 million. Melbourne surged past 243 million. Perth nearly doubled. Then, in a single year, it all collapsed — COVID-19 didn't just slow rail, it shattered it.

The patronage heatmap makes the scale of that collapse visceral: the cold amber columns of 2020-21 and 2021-22 stand in stark contrast to the amber warmth of the years before. Sydney lost nearly half its riders in two years. Melbourne lost two-thirds. Recovery has been slow and uneven — by 2022-23, neither city had returned to its pre-pandemic peak. But passenger volumes tell only half the story. Running a railway means managing not just schedules and crowds, but risk. The fatality heatmap beneath it shows that rail incidents — at level crossings, through trespass, and occasionally in collisions — continued regardless of how many people were on board. New South Wales and Victoria, with the country's largest and most complex networks, consistently record the most fatalities. In 2024, Sydney recorded 12 — the highest single-state total between 2012-2024.

Chapter 4 — The Reliability

The 40-Kilometre
Problem

A delayed city train is an inconvenience. A delayed regional train is a catastrophe. The difference is physics. Urban services stop every two kilometres — one broken-down train and the next simply waits a few minutes. Regional trains hurtle at 95 km/h with 40-kilometre gaps between stations. When something goes wrong on the Melbourne–Bendigo line, the delay does not stay local. It cascades, compounds, and by the time it reaches the city terminus, a single incident has swallowed an entire timetable.

The lollipop chart highlights the average journey speeds across different transit lines. The length of each bar reflects its speed in km/h, while the distinct colors separate rapid regional connectors from high-frequency urban networks.

Chapter 5 — The Freight

Moving
Mountains

While passenger trains fill the news, a different railway operates in silence. Australia's freight networks are among the most productive on Earth — hauling iron ore and coal in quantities that dwarf the world's busiest passenger systems. In the Pilbara, trains over 2.5 kilometres long run around the clock, seven days a week, in a rhythm as relentless as the tides.

The two stacked area charts below trace five years of freight exports by port for iron ore and coal — Australia's two dominant rail freight commodities. Port Hedland's band dominates the iron ore chart wholly. Coal tells a different story — Newcastle leads, but the total has been shrinking as global thermal coal demand softens.

Chapter 6 — The Gauge Wars

Three Colonies,
Three Mistakes

When Australia's colonies built their railways, each made its own choice — and none of them consulted each other. The result was three incompatible track widths baked permanently into the continent's infrastructure. For over a century, passengers travelling between major cities had to disembark at state borders and wait while their carriages were physically lifted and re-wheeled onto different bogies. A problem entirely of colonial stubbornness.

The timeline below traces the long, slow reckoning that followed — a unification story that took 141 years to get this far, and still is not finished.

The long road to gauge unification

1854
Victoria opens first railway on broad gauge, 1,600 mm.
1855
NSW switches to standard gauge — incompatible with Victoria from the start.
1865
Queensland adopts narrow gauge (1,067 mm) to cut construction costs.
1883
First major break-of-gauge occurs when NSW standard-gauge and Victorian broad-gauge lines meet at Albury.
1917
Trans-Australian railway opens, linking Perth to the east on standard gauge.
1921
Royal Commission recommends nationwide adoption of standard-gauge.
1962
Sydney-Melbourne converted to standard gauge — first direct service in 107 years.
1969
Standard gauge reaches Perth. The Indian Pacific begins running on a single gauge from Sydney to Perth.
1995
Melbourne-Adelaide converted: the interstate standard-gauge spine is finally complete.
Today
Queensland's break-of-gauge at the NSW border remains. Direct Sydney-Brisbane rail is still not possible.
Chapter 7 — The Investment

The Billion-Dollar
Building Boom

Australia is in the midst of its largest rail construction program in history. ABS data shows railway engineering construction reached AU$17 billion in 2023-24 — a historic high, and more than triple the 2015 figure of $5.2 billion.

The stream graph shows major sectors' investments from 1986 to 2025. The bump in the mid-2000s reflects the mining boom. The steep climb after 2015 reflects the urban megaprojects: Sydney Metro, METRONET, and Melbourne Airport Rail.
